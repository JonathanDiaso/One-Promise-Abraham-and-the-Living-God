# One Promise — The 15 Upgrades

A working edit plan. Each item gives what comes out, why, and what goes in instead.

---

## The diagnosis

**Your best material is in the callouts. Your prose spine carries the sermon.**

Neato Burrito. The three times Abraham handed Sarai away. The Etemenanki inscription. Eid al-Adha. The Pilate Stone. Rodney Stark's arithmetic. Tacitus. Simon of Cyrene. That is the material no other Abraham book can print — and 3,415 words of it sit in boxes beside the text, **22% of your body copy**, while the numbered paragraphs do theological connective tissue between them.

A reader skims boxes. You have put the unskippable material where readers skim.

The fix is not to write more. It is to move what you already have into the spine, and cut the connective tissue it replaces.

# The 15

## 1. Act Seven is a hole

**REMOVE** — nothing. There is nothing there to remove. That is the problem.

Act Seven is 3 paragraphs, 161 words. You open it with the strongest possible setup:

> "It was the last thing he asked for before he died. It is the one thing we have been worst at giving him."

Then you give the reader a timeline widget and move on.

**ADD** — the act you promised. Suggested beats:

1. **The first fracture was not doctrinal — it was ethnic.** You already built this in Acts 5–6 (Jerusalem Council, Acts 15). The church's first near-split was over whether Abraham's other children counted. Open Act Seven by connecting the fracture pattern to the covenant, not to church history trivia.
2. **1054.** Bread. Language. A letter left on an altar. Two men excommunicating each other in the name of the God who signed alone.
3. **1517.** What was recovered and what was lost in the recovering.
4. **The pattern paragraph.** You already have it in the callout — movement → institution → tradition replaces encounter → the Spirit drains, the form remains. Move that into the spine. It is the thesis of the act.
5. **Azusa Street.** You have this in P217 and it is the best thing in the act — Seymour learning God through a crack in a doorway because Jim Crow would not let him in the room, then carrying a movement of 600 million. Give it four paragraphs, not one.
6. **Land it on the cost.** Not "we should be unified." What the fractures *cost Ishmael's family* — which is exactly the question you raise in Act Eight and never answer. Act Seven is where that answer lives.

Target: 1,400–1,800 words. Same weight as Act Three.

## 2. Ken belongs in the spine, not a sidebar

**REMOVE** — the callout wrapper around "NEATO BURRITO — A STORY ABOUT THE GOD WHO SEES," and this dangling reference in P212 that assumes we already read it:

> "What happened to Ken has a name."

Right now a reader who skipped the box hits that sentence and has no idea who Ken is.

**ADD** — Ken as a full scene in the main text, placed exactly where the callout sits, running before P212. It is the only first-person, present-day, eyewitness material in 245 paragraphs. You were *there*. A stroke, migraines since age six, a daughter named Lilly who lived a few hours, a pastor at the bedside, and a man who never walked into a church again — then a burrito he never finished and a headache that stopped.

Every other miracle in this book is 2,000 or 4,000 years old and arrives with a citation. This one arrives with a witness. Put it in the spine and it becomes the moment the book stops being history.

## 3. Open on the firepot

**REMOVE** — the current opening position of Genesis 15. The covenant ceremony currently arrives around P045–P051, roughly 6,000 words in, after the entire Spirit preamble and most of Act One.

**ADD** — a cold open. Chapter one, first page:

> Abram cut the animals in half and laid them in two rows, the way the covenants were always cut. Both parties walk the aisle between the pieces. Both parties say the same thing without saying it: *may I become what these animals have become, if I break my word.*
>
> He drove off the birds. He waited. The sun went down, and a thick and dreadful darkness fell on him, and he slept.
>
> Only God walked.

*(Draft — make it yours.)*

Then: "This book is about what that means." Then go back and tell it from Ur.

This is your thesis rendered as an image, and it is currently buried. Nothing else you have competes with it as an opening.

**Knock-on:** the "Before We Embark — The Spirit" section (23 ¶, 1,005 words) becomes a throat-clear in front of a cold open. Compress it hard or relocate it behind Act One. You do not need to establish the Spirit's credentials before the reader has a reason to care.

## 4. Write Sarah

**REMOVE** — nothing directly. This is an absence, not a defect.

Sarah appears three times as an actor: she laughs (P090), she demands the expulsion (P091), she dies (P149). That is it. She held the promise for twenty-five years and has no interior life anywhere in the book.

**ADD** — one paragraph, placed at the top of Act Two, before Hagar. Give her what you gave Abraham in P117.

P117 is the best paragraph in the manuscript — *vayashkem Avraham baboker*, the word for loading a burden onto an animal's back before dawn, "a man who had stopped arguing with God at dawn." That paragraph works because you went into one Hebrew word and came out with a whole interior life.

Do that for Sarah. The material is right there: she is the one who has to hear the promise repeated every year, in her own tent, about her own body, and watch it not happen. Abraham gets to walk toward Moriah. Sarah gets to wait. The book currently treats her waiting as background to his.

## 5. Answer the question you raise in P220

**REMOVE** — nothing. Keep P220 exactly as written. It is one of the bravest paragraphs in the book:

> "Islam arose 600 years after the gospel had already been present in Ishmael's territory. The question of what happened in those 600 years — why the church failed to keep its presence among Abraham's other children — is one of the most sobering questions in church history."

**ADD** — the answer. Right now you raise it and move to the next section.

You have made the reader a promise. Christological controversies that fractured the Eastern churches. Chalcedon. The Arab Christian communities that were already there. Whatever your actual view is — state it. Two or three paragraphs.

This is also the natural bridge from Act Seven. The fractures did not just wound the church; they emptied the territory. Act Seven raises the cost, Act Eight names who paid it. That connection is the strongest structural argument available to you and it is currently missing.

## 6. Kill the hypothetical man in Tehran

**REMOVE** — P225 through P229, the second-person hypothetical:

> "Imagine you are a man in Tehran. You have never held a Bible… And then you have a dream."

**ADD** — one real, named, sourced conversion account.

You already cite David Garrison's *A Wind in the House of Islam* — over 1,000 former Muslims interviewed across 30 countries. Quote one of them. Name, place, what they saw, what it cost.

A hypothetical asks the reader to imagine. A named person with a citation does not ask — it reports. You are making the most contestable claim in the book (thousands of Iranians a year, dreams as the primary reported factor), and you are supporting it with an imagined scene plus statistics. One real witness is worth both.

Keep P229 — "He crossed a closed border, a death sentence, and 1,400 years of history to say one name in a dream" — as the landing after the real account.

## 7. Cut the guardrail in P052

**REMOVE** — P052 entirely:

> "But understand this clearly: the covenant was never designed to leave you where it found you. The same God who signed it alone is the God who sends His Spirit to transform from the inside. The promise does not excuse — it empowers. Grace is not permission to stay broken…"

**ADD** — nothing. Close the gap.

Look at what it interrupts. P049–P051 build to:

> "The covenant is only as breakable as God is breakable." → "Whatever you have done. Whatever has been done to you. The smoking firepot moved. The promise stands."

That is your peak. Then P052 arrives to make sure nobody misreads you as soft on sin. That is not the reader's worry — it is yours. It deflates the strongest moment in Act One to pre-empt a critic who is not in the room.

If the theological guardrail matters to you, it belongs later, in its own place, on its own terms. Not seventeen words after the firepot.

## 8. The five women are a montage

**REMOVE** — P106 through P110 as currently structured: Rebekah, Tamar, Ruth, Mary, Mary Magdalene, 60–140 words each, back to back, then P111 summarizing them ("Five women. Five different centuries. Five different forms of exclusion.").

**ADD** — one of them, at depth.

Two problems with the montage. First, it interrupts Hagar and Ishmael at the exact moment that story is landing, and never returns to her — even though P105 frames the whole section as "Hagar was the first woman to carry the promise… She was not the last." You open a frame and don't close it.

Second, five 90-word portraits is a list. Lists inform; they don't move anyone.

Take Tamar. She is the one your reader has never heard preached, the one Scripture refuses to soften, and the one Judah himself indicts himself over — "She is more righteous than I." Give her 600 words. Then return to Hagar and close the frame you opened.

Rebekah, Ruth, Mary, and Magdalene can each earn a single sentence in the closing move, or their own placement elsewhere. They should not be a queue.

## 9. Pay off Melchizedek

**REMOVE** — the Melchizedek half of P039, where he currently arrives mid-travelogue between a famine and a war:

> "…and on the way back was blessed by Melchizedek, a priest-king with no genealogy, no beginning and no end in the text… Remember that priest-king. He returns at the end of this story."

**ADD** — keep the hook, lose the full telling. In Act One reduce it to two sentences and the promise: a king with no father and no mother met him on the road, and we will come back to him.

Then in Act Eight, deliver. Currently the return is P237–P238 plus a truncated callout — and P237 re-narrates the whole scene as if for the first time ("Early in this story — before the covenant ceremony, before Isaac, before Moriah…"), which means the payoff has no surprise left in it. You spent the reveal in Act One and then spent it again.

Tell it *once*, at the end, at full length: a priesthood that preceded Israel, preceded the Levites, preceded every system that came after — bread and wine, in the name of God Most High, Creator of heaven and earth, served to one man on a road before there was a chosen nation to be outside of. That is the whole book's argument in one figure. It deserves the last full scene before the throne.

## 10. Abraham's betrayal belongs in the spine

**REMOVE** — the callout wrapper on "THREE TIMES — AND GOD SECURED IT ANYWAY," and the compressed treatment in P059.

**ADD** — the scene. Twice in the text — Pharaoh, then Abimelech — Abraham told his wife to say she was his sister, handed her to another man to save his own life, and walked away richer than he arrived.

You currently note this and move on in a single paragraph, then put the full accounting in a box. It is the most damning and most necessary material about your protagonist. It is also the thing that makes the covenant *mean* something: God bound himself alone to a man who traded away the woman the promise had to come through, and then did it again.

A reader who has not felt that cannot feel P047 — "Abram never walked through. Only God moved."

Put it in the spine, at length, before the Hagar section. Let it be uncomfortable.

## 11. End on the stars

**REMOVE** — P240 through P245 from the closing position, and the Proverbs 4:23 finish. *(Proverbs is already cut from the audio master.)*

Specifically, this is a second ending stacked on a first:

> P241: "One promise. One Spirit. Every nation."
> P242: "And the writer of Hebrews makes one final statement that changes everything…"
> P243–P245: three more closing paragraphs.
> Then: "Above all else, guard your heart."

**ADD** — P239 as the final paragraph of the book:

> "Abraham looked up at stars he couldn't count. God said: that is how many. John looked at a crowd he couldn't count. God said: those are them."

That is your last line. It closes the image you opened with, it needs no explanation, and everything currently following it is the book explaining a thing it already did. The Hebrews 11 material (P242–P243) is good — move it earlier, into Act Eight proper.

The Proverbs verse argues the opposite of your thesis. Your book says the covenant holds *because it does not depend on you*. Ending on a verse about self-vigilance undoes 245 paragraphs in one line.

## 12. Move the invitation to the end

**REMOVE** — P022–P023 from the preamble:

> "And if you want what fell in that upper room — Jesus said it simply… You can ask. Right now. That's all."
> "He has been waiting for you to ask. That was always the only thing he wanted."

**ADD** — the same words, at the very end, after the stars.

This is the book's actual ask, and it currently lands on roughly page four — before the reader has met Abraham, before the firepot, before Hagar names God, before Ken. You are asking for the decision before you have made the case.

Asked last, after 12,000 words of evidence, those same two paragraphs are the close of the book. Asked fourth, they are a tract.

## 13. Write two more scenes with dialogue

**REMOVE** — nothing. This is a technique you use once and abandon.

**ADD** — dialogue in two more places.

In 245 paragraphs there is exactly one real exchange:

> "Father?" — "Yes, my son?" — "The fire and the wood are here, but where is the lamb for the burnt offering?"

It is the most alive moment in the manuscript, and it is alive *because* two people speak. Everywhere else you narrate what was said.

Two obvious candidates:

- **Hagar at the spring.** You already have the angel's questions — "Where have you come from, and where are you going?" She answers nothing in your telling. Let her answer.
- **Abraham at the firepot.** "What can you give me since I remain childless?" is already a line of dialogue in P041. Build the scene around it rather than reporting it.

## 14. Move Ethiopia before Philip

**REMOVE** — P201–P203 from their current position *after* the eunuch's baptism, where they read as an appendix that arrives once the scene is over.

**ADD** — the same three paragraphs, relocated to just *before* Philip runs to the chariot. Queen of Sheba, "the half was not told me," Jesus citing her himself, the Kebra Nagast, Menelik, Psalm 68:31 as a 1,700-year-old national calling.

Same words. Zero new writing. Enormous difference.

Placed first, the eunuch is not a stranger encountering the covenant — he is a nation's thousand-year thread being picked back up on a desert road. Your own line already says it: "The eunuch was a reconnection." That sentence is currently *after* the thing it reframes.

## 15. Dramatize young Abram

**REMOVE** — the summary treatment in P029:

> "Young Abram looked at the sun and reasoned: something made that. He looked at the moon and the stars: something made those too… He worked his way through every created thing — fire, water, clouds, wind — and arrived at the uncreated Creator by logic."

**ADD** — the scene. A boy in his father's idol shop, who knows every god by name, knows what each one is supposed to do, knows the prayers and the going rate — and starts watching the sky instead. Then, one night, burns the shop down.

This is your origin story and you summarize it in a paragraph that begins by citing the Book of Jubilees and the Dead Sea Scrolls. The sourcing is good and should stay — as a note. The scene should be a scene.

You have the detail already: Terah sold gods for a living, so Abram grew up in inventory. A child who handles the merchandise is the one least likely to believe in it. That is the whole conversion, and it is sitting in P027–P028 as background.

## What this does to the shape

Net effect if you do all fifteen: the preamble shrinks, Act One opens on its own thesis, Acts Four and Seven stop being sidebar-heavy, the callouts empty into the spine, and the book ends on the image it opened with.

You would be moving roughly 2,000 words out of boxes and into the text, cutting perhaps 800 words of connective tissue, and writing maybe 2,500 new — most of it in Act Seven.

That is not a rewrite. That is a re-weighting. The book is already here. It is just distributed wrong.
